A crowd of penguins stands at the edge of the ice. They're hungry, and the fish are right there, below them. But not one of them moves.

[SHOT: The dark water under the ice edge, still and quiet]
They shuffle. They lean forward. They step back again. They're waiting for someone else to go first, because somewhere in that dark water, something may be waiting too.

[SHOT: A leopard seal gliding underwater]
It's long, it's silver, and it's patient. Meet the leopard seal, the top hunter of the Antarctic coast, and one of the strangest predators on the planet.

## What Is A Leopard Seal
[SHOT: The seal resting on an ice floe]
The leopard seal's scientific name is Hydrurga leptonyx. It's a true seal, part of the same family as harbor seals and elephant seals. That means it has no ear flaps, just small openings on the side of its head. [VERIFY: family and ear openings]

[SHOT: Map of the Southern Ocean with Antarctica highlighted]
It lives mostly in the pack ice that surrounds Antarctica, a vast and shifting world of floating ice that grows every winter and shrinks every summer. [VERIFY: range]

[SHOT: Close view of the spotted coat]
And the name? It comes from its coat. A leopard seal is silver-gray on top, paler underneath, and covered in dark spots, a little like a big cat. [VERIFY: coloring]

## A Giant With A Twist
[SHOT: The seal beside a human silhouette for scale]
Leopard seals are big. The largest reach around 3.5 meters, about 11 feet, and can weigh close to half a ton. That's roughly as heavy as seven grown men. [VERIFY: maximum length and weight]

[SHOT: Two seals side by side, the female larger]
And here's the twist. In many animals, males are the bigger sex. With leopard seals, it's the other way around. The biggest leopard seals are females. [VERIFY: females larger than males]

[SHOT: The seal's huge head in profile]
Then there's the head. It's enormous for a seal, with a long snout and a wide mouth that stretches back past the eyes. Some people say it looks almost like a reptile. [VERIFY: head and gape]

## Built For Speed
[SHOT: The seal swimming fast, front flippers sweeping]
Most true seals swim by sweeping their back flippers side to side. Leopard seals often power through the water with their long front flippers instead, almost like wings. [VERIFY: front-flipper propulsion]

[SHOT: The seal turning sharply around an ice block]
Their bodies are slender and flexible, which makes them fast and agile. They can twist, turn and accelerate in a burst, exactly what you need when your prey is a quick, slippery penguin. [VERIFY: agility]

[SHOT: The seal diving down into deeper blue water]
Most of their dives are fairly shallow, but they can hold their breath for many minutes when they need to. [VERIFY: dive depth and duration]

## Two Mouths In One
[SHOT: The seal's head with its jaws open]
Now let's look inside that mouth, because it hides one of nature's neatest tricks. At the front are long, sharp canine teeth, perfect for grabbing and holding prey. [VERIFY: canines]

[SHOT: Diagram of the interlocking cheek teeth]
But further back are the cheek teeth. Each one has three sharp points. When the jaws close, those points lock together and leave tiny gaps between them, like the holes in a kitchen strainer. [VERIFY: three-pointed cheek teeth]

[SHOT: A swarm of krill drifting in blue water]
That lets a leopard seal take a big gulp of water full of krill, the tiny shrimp-like animals that swarm in the Southern Ocean, and then push the water back out while the krill stay inside. [VERIFY: krill filtering]

[SHOT: A whale silhouette passing behind the krill swarm]
Think about that. The same animal that hunts penguins can also feed a little like a whale. One mouth, two completely different ways to eat. [VERIFY: krill as a significant food at times]

## The Menu
[SHOT: Krill, fish and squid icons appearing one by one]
So what does a leopard seal actually eat? Pretty much whatever the season offers. Krill can make up a big part of the diet at certain times of the year. Fish and squid are on the menu too. [VERIFY: diet composition]

[SHOT: A penguin swimming above the seal]
Then there are the penguins. A penguin is fast underwater, but a leopard seal only has to win once. [VERIFY: penguins as prey]

[SHOT: A crabeater seal with long scars]
And leopard seals also hunt other seals, especially young ones. Many crabeater seals carry long scars on their bodies, thought to come from leopard seal attacks they survived when they were young. [VERIFY: crabeater scars]

[SHOT: The seal shaking its prey at the surface]
Leopard seals can't chew big prey into small bites. So with a large catch, they often shake it hard at the surface to tear it into pieces they can swallow. [VERIFY: shaking behavior]

## Ambush At The Ice Edge
[SHOT: The seal waiting just below the surface near the ice]
The leopard seal's favorite weapon is patience. It often lingers close to the ice edge, right where penguins have to enter and leave the water. [VERIFY: ice-edge ambush]

[SHOT: The seal's head barely breaking the surface]
It can wait quietly near the surface, hard to spot from above. To a penguin standing on the ice, the water may look completely empty. [VERIFY]

[SHOT: Penguins diving in together while the seal turns]
That's why penguins hesitate. And when they finally jump, they often go together. A crowd of birds splashing in at once is much harder to target than one penguin alone. [VERIFY: group entry]

[SHOT: Penguins leaping out onto the ice]
The same danger waits on the way home. Coming back out of the water, penguins often launch themselves onto the ice as fast as they can. [VERIFY]

## Songs Under The Ice
[SHOT: Sound waves rippling through the dark water]
Here's something most people never hear. Leopard seals sing. [VERIFY: vocalizations]

[SHOT: A male seal hanging head-down in the water]
During the breeding season, males produce long, low, eerie calls underwater, repeating them again and again. To human ears, they sound almost like something from science fiction. [VERIFY: male breeding calls]

[SHOT: A hydrophone listening station on the ice]
Scientists record these songs with underwater microphones. Because leopard seals are so hard to find and count, listening to them is one of the best ways to learn where they are. [VERIFY: acoustic monitoring]

## Life On The Ice
[SHOT: A single seal resting alone on a floe]
Leopard seals are mostly loners. You'll usually see them alone, resting on an ice floe between hunts. [VERIFY: solitary]

[SHOT: A mother and pup on the pack ice]
Females give birth on the pack ice, usually in the southern summer. The pup nurses for a few weeks, then has to learn to hunt for itself. [VERIFY: pupping season and nursing]

[SHOT: A killer whale silhouette in the distance]
As adults, leopard seals have very few natural enemies. Killer whales are the main one. [VERIFY: predators]

[SHOT: The seal sleeping on the ice under a pale sky]
In the wild, leopard seals can live for more than two decades. [VERIFY: lifespan]

## Meeting Humans
[SHOT: A wooden ship trapped in sea ice]
People have been meeting leopard seals for a long time. In 1916, during Ernest Shackleton's famous expedition, the crew were camped on drifting sea ice after their ship was crushed. [VERIFY: Endurance expedition]

[SHOT: A man running across the ice, a seal bursting from the water]
One crew member, Thomas Orde-Lees, was chased across the ice by a leopard seal. Another expedition member shot the animal before it could reach him. [VERIFY: Orde-Lees encounter]

[SHOT: A diver underwater with a seal circling at a distance]
Today, divers and scientists meet leopard seals far more often. And most of those encounters are about curiosity. The seal circles, watches, opens its mouth, and eventually swims away. [VERIFY: most encounters are curiosity]

[SHOT: The seal pushing a penguin toward a diver]
Some are downright strange. One wildlife photographer described a female that kept bringing him penguins, again and again, as if she was trying to feed him. [VERIFY: Paul Nicklen encounter]

[SHOT: A quiet Antarctic research station at dusk]
But leopard seals are powerful wild predators, and that should never be forgotten. In 2003, a leopard seal killed British marine biologist Kirsty Brown while she was snorkeling near her research station. [VERIFY: 2003 incident]

[SHOT: The research station shoreline, calm water]
It's the only known human death caused by the species. It led to stricter safety rules for scientists working in Antarctic waters, including closer watch for seals before anyone enters the water. [VERIFY: rule changes]

## Wanderers
[SHOT: Map with dotted paths from Antarctica to New Zealand and Australia]
Leopard seals don't always stay in the far south. Young seals in particular sometimes wander thousands of kilometers north. [VERIFY: vagrants]

[SHOT: A seal resting on a harbor beach]
They've turned up on the coasts of New Zealand, Australia, South America and South Africa. In New Zealand, they're now seen often enough that some are treated as residents rather than rare visitors. [VERIFY: New Zealand status]

[SHOT: A small boat in a harbor with a seal nearby]
When a leopard seal hauls out on a busy beach, the advice is simple: give it plenty of space, and keep dogs well away. [VERIFY: public advice]

## How Scientists Study Them
[SHOT: A small research boat weaving between ice floes]
Studying an animal that lives scattered across a frozen ocean is hard work. Researchers often start by simply searching for seals resting on the ice, from boats, ships and aircraft. [VERIFY: survey methods]

[SHOT: A seal wearing a small satellite tag on its head]
Some leopard seals have been fitted with small tags that track where they travel and how deep they dive. When the seal molts, the tag falls off with the old fur. [VERIFY: tags and molt]

[SHOT: A tiny camera on the seal's back, seen from the side]
Others have carried tiny cameras, giving scientists a seal's-eye view of how it searches, chases and catches its food. [VERIFY: animal-borne cameras]

[SHOT: A scientist examining samples in a lab]
And some of the best clues are hiding in what the seals leave behind. By studying their droppings, scientists can identify bones, beaks and even DNA from the animals they ate. [VERIFY: scat analysis]

## Myths And Facts
[SHOT: Big bold text: MYTH vs FACT, with the seal behind]
Leopard seals have a scary reputation, so let's clear up a few myths. [VERIFY]

[SHOT: The seal with a big question mark]
Myth number one: leopard seals mostly eat penguins. In reality, their diet changes with the season and the place. Krill, fish and other seals can matter just as much. [VERIFY: diet varies]

[SHOT: A diver with a circling seal, a green check mark]
Myth number two: every leopard seal that approaches a diver is attacking. Most are curious. But because a real attack can come without warning, divers still treat every encounter seriously. [VERIFY]

[SHOT: The seal on the ice with a polar bear silhouette crossed out]
And myth number three: leopard seals and polar bears might meet. They never do. Polar bears live in the Arctic, at the top of the planet. Leopard seals live in the Antarctic, at the bottom. [VERIFY: no overlap]

## A Changing Ice
[SHOT: Sea ice breaking apart]
Leopard seals depend on sea ice. They rest on it, give birth on it, and molt on it. And their food web depends on it too, especially the krill that so many Antarctic animals rely on. [VERIFY: reliance on sea ice and krill]

[SHOT: Satellite-style view of shrinking ice around Antarctica]
In recent years, Antarctic sea ice has reached some of its lowest levels on record. Scientists are watching closely to understand what that means for animals like the leopard seal. [VERIFY: recent sea ice lows]

[SHOT: A researcher counting seals from a small boat]
The surprising part? Nobody knows exactly how many leopard seals there are. They live scattered across a huge, icy ocean, which makes them one of the hardest seals on Earth to count. [VERIFY: population uncertainty]
